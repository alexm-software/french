# Verbs and their representation

Read before planning, painting or reviewing any verb, verb card or verb street.

## Choosing how verbs are represented (decide per language)

How verbs look is a design decision for each target language and culture. The French build's mime is one example, not the default. In the representation spec, before painting: <!-- L43 -->

1. **List what a learner must tell apart** in that language: verbs from nouns at a glance, plus the verb features worth encoding (conjugation class or group, irregularity, reflexive or middle voice, aspect pairs such as Russian or Polish perfective and imperfective, separable prefixes in German or Dutch, Arabic root patterns, Japanese ichidan, godan and irregular classes, transitivity, measure-word or particle patterns). <!-- L44 -->
2. **Pick one recurring verb marker that's culturally at home in the target country** and that nobody else in the world resembles. Examples: a mime in a striped shirt for French; a flamenco dancer for Spanish; a Bavarian brass-band musician or a craftsman in a Wanderjahr outfit for German; a Peking-opera performer or a tai-chi practitioner for Mandarin; a ballet dancer for Russian; a calligrapher-storyteller (*hakawati*) for Arabic; a kabuki actor or a *kuroko* stagehand for Japanese. Avoid stereotypes or caricature, religious figures, uniforms of armed forces, and anything a native speaker could find mocking. Check with someone who knows the culture when unsure. <!-- L45 -->
3. **Give each verb feature its own visual channel on that marker** (one costume item's colour for the class, a held prop for reflexive, a pair of matching figures or a before/after pose for aspect pairs, a detachable badge for a separable prefix). Keep these channels clear of the gender and placement cues. <!-- L46 -->
4. **The marker acts inside the room's scene** with the room's nouns, never bunched with other verbs (see "Verbs live inside the scenes"). A troupe of identical-costume markers is fine when one room has many verbs. <!-- L47 -->
5. **Show a sample to the user and get sign-off** on the marker and its code before bulk painting. Record the choice in the project plan. <!-- L48 -->
6. **When a marker doesn't fit at all** (for example, a language with few inflected verbs), a simpler cue can carry it, such as a motion-line halo or a small consistent hand icon, as long as verbs stay easy to spot and are still acted out. <!-- L49 -->

## French reference build

French reference build: "Monsieur Verbe", a cheerful mime in a striped shirt, suspenders and white gloves with motion lines (approved by the user). A beret's colour encodes the group (red -er, green -ir, purple -re, gold irregular), chosen to avoid the gender label colours, and a hand mirror marks pronominal verbs. Example: *manger* is Monsieur Verbe in a red beret eating lunch in a donkey's manger (the card notes it's a spelling link, said mahn-ZHAY). Mix verbs into the themed rooms where the action happens (cutting in the kitchen, leaving at the station). <!-- L51 -->

## Where verbs go

- **Verbs** sit in the ordinary themed rooms, acted out by the language's chosen verb marker (see "Choosing how verbs are represented"). Governed prepositions and constructions (e.g. *aller à*, *venir de*) go on the card and may be hinted with a small prop. <!-- L55 -->
- Most verbs belong in the themed rooms where their action naturally happens, spread across the scene among the nouns and doing something with them: kneading dough on the baker's table, haggling over the scale at a stall, carrying a trunk up the stairs. Keep the verb marker's cue so a verb is still easy to spot. <!-- L131 -->
- **Avoid pushing verbs aside:** don't bunch a room's verbs into one corner or zone, or line them along an edge (the room fails), and don't put nearly all verbs on separate streets where learners walk past them. A room's verbs are spread through the scene and interact with its nouns; verb streets are allowed where they make sense, but most of a sector's verbs sit in its themed rooms. <!-- L132 G141 -->
- This is a balance, not an absolute. A verb street or a cluster of related actions is fine where it genuinely makes sense (a removals street for carrying and lifting verbs, a dance hall for dance moves), as long as most verbs are part of the scenes. <!-- L133 -->
- When repainting a building, bring the verbs from its sector's verb street that fit its rooms into those rooms, and keep the street only if what's left still makes a real scene. <!-- L134 -->
- Verbs: the verb character acts the sound-alike inside a natural scene for the street's setting, with props that carry the sound (a rat for rattacher, a chariot for charrier). <!-- L114 -->

## Verb streets (superseded)

Separate walk-through verb streets and alleys, with -er verbs on one side and -ir/-re on the other, were tried and dropped by the user: too many places that blurred together, and the sides clashed with the gender sides. Use the verb character in themed rooms instead. <!-- L96 -->

## Verb card

Show tenses as tabs on the word popup. Conjugation tables for at least present, a common past (e.g. passé composé and imperfect for French), future, conditional, imperative, and present subjunctive, plus participles, auxiliary verb, and prepositions, with the verb character's colour code shown beside them. Generate tables with a script from a rules engine plus an exceptions list, then spot-check irregulars against an open reference. <!-- L66 -->
