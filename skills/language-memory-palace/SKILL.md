# Language memory palace

Layer this on top of the Memory palace builder skill, which covers build order, style slider, room checks, and tooling. This adds the language-specific rules.

## Scope and levels
- Cover the target levels (e.g. CEFR A1 to C2). Tag every word with its level so paths and quizzes can filter by level.
- Build the inventory from frequency lists and level-based syllabi under open licenses; record sources and attributions in a credits file.
- Level lists are noisy (everyday words can be graded C2). Cross-check level with corpus frequency and boost concrete everyday words; spot-check samples per level.
- Keep the full word list the user asked for, rare words included, but you may defer drawing assets for the rarest words: give them a home (room or zoom) and a card with no picture until later. Leave out only obsolete words and narrow professional jargon unless the user wants them.
- Organize by level: the core village holds beginner to intermediate words; themed advanced words go on an upper floor or back room of the same themed building; general abstract or academic advanced words may go in a temporary advanced area (e.g. a town across the bridge with a library, faculties, law court and museum), with the aim of eventually folding them into the main village. Never create numbered filler rooms; every room needs a memorable concrete theme, and a check should flag words that don't fit their room.

## Mnemonic rule (keyword method)
Each word is drawn as something that, said aloud in the learner's native language, sounds like the target word, and which also depicts the word's meaning. Example for French with English speakers: *la croix* ("krwah") is a crow perched on a cross. Always write the pronunciation hint and a one-line explanation of the mnemonic in the detail card. Check the sound match by saying both aloud and reject anything crude or stretched. Prefer sound links; a spelling-only link (e.g. *manger* and English "manger") is acceptable when no good sound-alike exists, as long as the card says it's a spelling link and gives the real pronunciation.

## Hook quality bar (language)
- The sound-alike must be a real word or phrase in the learner's language that sounds like the target word said aloud, at least the stressed or first syllable, and it must appear in the scene. Write the meaning in CAPS and the pronunciation in brackets.
- Examples (French to English) of weak hooks: soif shown as just a thirsty camel, with no sound link; froidement as "fried man", which is too far from the real sound. A better one: soif as a thirsty camel SWADDLED in wet towels.
- Never use the meaning as the sound-alike. 'cook' for cuisinier or 'cat' for chat (said SHAH) link nothing to the French sound. Derive the sound-alike from the pronunciation (IPA or respelling) and check it out loud.
- Cognates count only when the word both looks AND sounds close (sauce, soupe). Write them plainly ("Same as English 'sauce', said SOHS") with one vivid image that fits the room, and note any pronunciation trap. Words that look alike but sound different (frite, said FREET) still need a sound cue. Mark false friends explicitly on the card.
- Cognates still need a playful, memorable twist, ideally one that encodes the target-language pronunciation where it differs. A flat "like English X: someone uses X" line is not a mnemonic. Paint the twist.
- Never drop a word for lack of a perfect hook; a plain, appealing depiction of the item is fine.
- No template or meta wording on cards ("Picture the plain object", "label glowing"). Every hook is a specific scene, and both the sound cue and the meaning are visibly painted.
- Before bulk generation, write and get sign-off on a sample room of hooks. After generation, audit by reading each hook aloud against its respelling. Never trust a generator's own strength rating.
- Watch automatic glosses for vulgar senses (dictionary data often lists slang first) and replace them with the everyday meaning.
- But do warn the learner: give any word with a vulgar, offensive, or insulting meaning a usage_warning on its card. Word it age-appropriately ("Careful: also has a vulgar slang meaning") without spelling out the crude sense.

## Representation design (decide before painting anything)
Deciding how each kind of item is represented is part of building the palace, not an afterthought. Before any room is painted, write a short representation spec, show the user a drawn sample of each device, and get sign-off. Cover at least:
- **Each word class** (nouns, verbs, adjectives, adverbs, function words, phrases): how it is drawn and how a learner can tell its class at a glance in a mixed room.
- **Each grammatical feature worth remembering** (gender, verb group or irregularity, reflexive, plural quirks): which single visual cue encodes it. Give every feature its own channel (position, a costume item's colour, a prop) and never reuse one channel for two features. For example, if left and right already mean gender, don't also use sides for verb group.
- **Items that don't need a mnemonic each** (alphabet letters, number lists, article tables, sound rules): one reference prop such as a bulletin board, poster or blackboard in the beginner area, not a drawn mnemonic per letter.
- **Rare or advanced words**: where they live (zooms, upper floors, an advanced area) and whether their pictures are deferred.
- **Sensitive words**: as text on a museum wall, never drawn.
Check every device against the others for clashes, put the device's meaning on the word card (for example a beret icon next to the conjugation), and record the decisions in the project plan so every later room follows them.

### Proven device for verbs: one recurring verb character
Mix verbs into the ordinary themed rooms where the action naturally happens (cutting in the kitchen, leaving at the station, praying in the church) instead of separate verb streets. Make them unmistakable by having every verb acted out by one recurring character that nobody else in the world resembles. For French this was "Monsieur Verbe", a cheerful mime in a striped shirt, suspenders and white gloves with motion lines (approved by the user). One costume item encodes the verb group through its colour, and the colours avoid the gender label colours: a red beret for -er, green for -ir, purple for -re and gold for irregular verbs. A hand mirror marks reflexive or pronominal verbs. The scene around him carries the hook (French example: *manger* is Monsieur Verbe in a red beret eating lunch in a donkey's manger; the card notes it's a spelling link, since it's said mahn-ZHAY). A troupe of identical-costume characters is fine when one room has many verbs. Adapt the character and the colour code to the target language's verb classes.

## Placement grammar (adapt to the language)
- **Grammatical gender**: feminine nouns clearly on the left half of the room, masculine clearly on the right (a middle zone for neuter where the language has it). Don't put a big physical divider or object splitting the room; an item near the middle may overhang the centre line slightly, but its main body sits on its side. Zoom spots are their own small rooms and follow the same rule. Don't tint the walls; optionally color the word's label text by gender.
- **Verbs** sit in the ordinary themed rooms, acted out by the recurring verb character (see Representation design). Governed prepositions and constructions (e.g. *aller à*, *venir de*) go on the card and may be hinted with a small prop.
- **Adjectives, adverbs, opposites, emotions, idioms**: place them in themed rooms where they fit, or in their own rooms when there's no natural home; opposites can face each other across a room.
- **Mature or sensitive words** go as text on a museum wall, never drawn.

## Room density
Each room's main painting holds about 10 to 12 of its commonest words, well spaced, with the verbs among them. Add zoom spots (a counter, shelf, desk or stall of about 6 words each) only where the room needs more space and the spot makes sense; there is no fixed number. Rare words can wait in a room or zoom as cards without pictures.

## Detail card for every word
Every word must have at least one natural example sentence with a translation, using the word in its main sense. Also include: definition in your own words, pronunciation (IPA plus a plain respelling) and a play button using the browser's built-in speech synthesis with a target-language voice, gender and article, plural, mnemonic explanation, false-cognate warnings, common phrases and idioms, example sentences, and level.

## Verb card
Show tenses as tabs on the word popup. Conjugation tables for at least present, a common past (e.g. passé composé and imperfect for French), future, conditional, imperative, and present subjunctive, plus participles, auxiliary verb, and prepositions, with the verb character's colour code shown beside them. Generate tables with a script from a rules engine plus an exceptions list, then spot-check irregulars against an open reference.

## Beginner area (the starting point)
Greetings, farewells, polite phrases, the alphabet (on one bulletin board or poster, not a mnemonic per letter), pronunciation and sound rules, articles, numbers, pronouns, basic grammar rules, and general how-the-language-works tips. Grammar-rule rooms live here (for a village, the school).

## Thematic sectors (adapt to the culture)
Nature (beach, mountain or ski resort, campsite, farm), university (sciences, math, engineering, computing, humanities), religious sites (churches with the user's chosen prayers in the target language, shown as blended wall or plaque text with a translation toggle and audio, plus other faiths' temples treated respectfully; follow the reverence rules in the Memory palace builder skill), home, bank, shops and market, travel (station, airport, hotel), health, government, restaurants and cafes. Pick real cultural details of the target country for authenticity.

## Conversations
Each room gets one or more short dialogues using its words, playable with browser speech synthesis (one voice per speaker where available), with a transcript and translation toggle.
- Dialogues must be realistic everyday scenes (ordering at a café, booking a table, cooking with a friend, asking a waiter about the bill) with natural turns, using the room's words in context.
- Never generate them from a template, and never have characters quiz each other ("Do you know the verb X? It means Y"). If a room has no hand-written dialogue yet, hide the conversation rather than show filler.

## Labels
Every placed item has a label (word with article). Offer a header switch, remembered between visits, between labels always shown (default) and labels on hover or tap only.

## Interface language
Give all site UI and meta text (guide, place names and descriptions, labels, tooltips, page title) a native/target-language toggle, keeping the strings in one i18n file. Vocabulary content (words, glosses, example pairs, mnemonics) stays as it is.

## Quiz modes
Image to word, word to image, gender (left or right), verb group (which beret colour), conjugation fill-in, listen and choose, and room recall. Filter by room, sector, or level.

## Extra language checks per room (in addition to the generic room checks)
- Gender placement is correct for every noun.
- Articles, accents, and spelling are correct (validate with a script against the data file).
- Mnemonic truly connects the native-language sound (or, where flagged, the spelling) to the target meaning.
- False cognates are flagged.
- Conjugation tables are complete for every verb in the room, and each verb is acted by the verb character with the right colour code.

## Verb streets (superseded)
Separate walk-through verb streets and alleys, with -er verbs on one side and -ir/-re on the other, were tried and dropped by the user: too many places that blurred together, and the sides clashed with the gender sides. Use the verb character in themed rooms instead.

## Validation (language-specific checks for validator bots)
Run these in addition to the generic Validation section of the Memory palace builder skill.
- **Words:** spelling, accents and grammatical properties (gender, class, etc. where the language has them) are correct. Each word sits where the project's placement scheme says a word with its properties belongs (check zoom spots too).
- **Glosses:** they are accurate and cover the common sense first. They are written in our own words or properly credited.
- **Example sentences:** every word has one, in natural, grammatical target-language text that actually uses the word in the sense being taught. The translation is faithful.
- **Verb cards:** every tense tab is present and the conjugations are correct (spot-check irregular verbs fully). The verb character's colour code (and reflexive mirror) matches the verb's class.
- **Conversations:** they are realistic everyday dialogues, with no template or quiz lines. Each `used_words` entry really appears in the lines. Unwritten conversations are hidden.
- **Mnemonics:** the sound link works for a native speaker of the learner's language. Hooks contain no crude or slang sound-alikes.
- **Pronunciation:** if there is audio or speech synthesis, check that the target-language voice is used.
- **Quoted texts:** prayers and other quoted texts use the public-domain or licensed wording recorded in the audit. Required entries are present, and similar texts are clearly named.

## Hook lessons (user review, Oct 25)
- Build each hook from an English sound-alike of the whole word, then paint it literally: talus is a tall loo, rocher is rowing through snow, grandir is a grand deer, abaisser is the letters A, B and C (ah-bay-say) being lowered, vieillir is an old man cheering "yay, a year".
- Never lean on another word in the same language (piéger cannot be shown with a piège) or on knowing the target language.
- For abstract or quantity words, show the meaning and the sound together (le grain is a microscope on a tiny grain beside wheat).
- Keep homonyms out of a themed room unless the room fits that sense (tronc the trunk belongs in the forest, tronc the church box does not).
- Verbs: the verb character acts the sound-alike inside a natural scene for the street's setting, with props that carry the sound (a rat for rattacher, a chariot for charrier).

## Placement and hook lessons (outside review, Oct 25)
- **Place by meaning, never by a shared English gloss word.** Most misfits came from this: berge (river *bank*) in the bank, éleveur (*stock*breeder) with investments, polonais (*Polish*) in the repair shop, licencier (to *fire* someone) next to embraser (to set on *fire*), cocher (*coach*man) in the stadium. Run a placement check that flags any word whose only tie to its room is a shared gloss word (bank, stock, firm, coach, fire, party, star, band, scale, lead, shot, stage, league, race, game, trump, fair, parcel, shell, plain, heading, handle).
- **Gloss the sense the room teaches.** A forest teaches feuille as *leaf*, not "sheet of paper"; a sky room teaches éclair as *lightning*, not the pastry; a sacristy teaches aube as the *alb*. When one spelling has two senses in two rooms (éclair, serviette, caisse, crêpe, croissant, facteur, cascade, régime), give each sense its own word id, card and hook, so each one can't be mistaken for the other.
- **Grave or sensitive words never sit in light-hearted rooms** (no suicide verb on a "Relief Street", no killing in a hair salon). They go to a sober text gallery as plain wall text, or are dropped.
- **No overflow rooms.** A leftover "stump", "attic" or "rack" room filled with unrelated abstract nouns can't be painted as a scene. Either give the room an honest abstract theme and paint it as one, or move the words to buildings whose theme fits (humanities, philosophy, problems rooms). Themed buildings get their real themed words (a sewing room teaches coudre, fil, aiguille, ourlet).
- **Phrase-only words are taught as their phrase** (en avoir marre, ne… guère, clin d'œil, lors de, afin de, au sein de), and verbs that are only ever reflexive are shown with *se*.
- **One hook per word, everywhere.** A word placed in two rooms shares one hook; only the setting adapts. Prefer one home per word.
- **What makes a pun forced:** the sound is off (wrong vowel), there are too many pieces (one chunk on the stressed syllable beats four chunks covering every sound), the pun object only sits *beside* the meaning instead of acting on it, there's no pun at all (just the meaning acted out), or the wrong template is used (calling a non-cognate "like English X"). Good model: two chunks and one action, with one person or one animal.
- **Use only English the learner knows.** No Latin or word-history hooks, and no obscure English ("chandler"). A French word the learner has certainly met already is fine as a helper.
- **A real cognate may stay plain** when no natural pun turns up; note any false-friend trap (combustible means *fuel*).
- **Adverbs:** where an -ment adverb's own pun is weak, reuse the adjective's pun plus "-ly".
- **A moved word needs its hook and painting redone**, because hooks describe the room they're painted in.
- **Inner areas match the overview.** Every district's sector map, building exteriors and interiors keep the landmarks, architecture, materials and colours the top-level map shows for it.

## Verbs live inside the scenes (user review, Oct 25 evening)
- Don't cluster verbs: no verb corner, no row of verb mimes along one edge, no separate verb street or alley. Grouping pushes them aside, so learners miss them.
- Each verb sits in the room where its action naturally happens, spread across the scene among the nouns and doing something with them: the verb character kneads the dough on the baker's table, haggles over the scale at the stall, or carries the trunk up the stairs. Keep the verb character's costume cue so a verb is still easy to spot.
- When a building is repainted, move the verbs from its sector's verb street into its rooms where they fit, and retire the emptied street or give it a real non-verb purpose.
